# Named Entity Recognition (NER) in NLP — Detailed Study Notes

Topic 3 · Natural Language Processing · Fundamentals, entity extraction, spaCy, NLTK, Transformers, and practical implementation

## 1. What is Named Entity Recognition?

**Named Entity Recognition (NER)** is an NLP task that identifies important entities in text and classifies them into predefined categories, such as people, organizations, locations, dates, and monetary values.

For example, consider this sentence\:

> Sundar Pichai became the CEO of Google in 2015 and works in California.

An NER system might produce\:

| Entity | Entity label | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Sundar Pichai | PERSON | Name of a person |
| Google | ORG | Organization |
| 2015 | DATE | Date or year |
| California | GPE | Geopolitical entity |

Notice that NER does two things\:

1. **Entity detection\:** Finds the relevant text span.
2. **Entity classification\:** Assigns a category to that span.

For example, identifying `Google` is entity detection, while assigning it the label `ORG` is entity classification.

NER is also called **entity extraction** in many practical NLP workflows, although information extraction is a broader field that includes extracting relationships and events as well.

## 2. Why is NER important?

A computer can store a sentence as text without necessarily identifying which parts represent people, places, organizations, or other real-world entities.

NER converts unstructured text into structured information that other applications can use.

For example\:

> Apple announced a new product in California on Monday.

An NER system could identify\:

- `Apple` → Organization
- `California` → Location
- `Monday` → Date

A downstream system can then store these entities in a database or use them for document analysis.

### Major applications of NER

**1\. News analysis**

Extract politicians, companies, locations, and dates from news articles to organize and search stories.

---

**2\. Healthcare and biomedical NLP**

Identify medication names, diseases, procedures, and other medical concepts using appropriate domain-specific models.

---

**3\. Customer support**

Extract product names, company names, dates, and other relevant information from customer messages.

---

**4\. Knowledge graphs and search**

Extract entities from documents and connect them with relationships to build structured knowledge.

Other applications include resume parsing, financial document analysis, legal document processing, question answering, recommendation systems, and automated data entry.

## 3. Types of named entities

Different NER datasets and models define different entity categories. The following are common categories used in general-purpose English NER.

### 3.1 PERSON

Identifies people and sometimes fictional characters, depending on the annotation guidelines.

Examples\:

- Suman Pun
- Sundar Pichai
- Marie Curie
- Albert Einstein

Sentence\:

> Marie Curie studied radioactivity.

Entity\:

`Marie Curie` → `PERSON`

### 3.2 ORG — Organization

Identifies companies, institutions, agencies, and other organizations.

Examples\:

- Google
- Microsoft
- World Health Organization
- Tribhuvan University

Sentence\:

> Microsoft opened an office in Nepal.

Entity\:

`Microsoft` → `ORG`

### 3.3 GPE — Geopolitical Entity

Identifies countries, states, cities, and similar political or administrative regions.

Examples\:

- Nepal
- Kathmandu
- California
- Japan

Sentence\:

> Kathmandu is the capital of Nepal.

Entities\:

- `Kathmandu` → `GPE`
- `Nepal` → `GPE`

### 3.4 LOC — Location

Identifies geographical locations that are not necessarily geopolitical entities.

Examples can include mountain ranges, bodies of water, and geographical regions.

Sentence\:

> The expedition crossed the Himalayas.

Entity\:

`Himalayas` → `LOC`

The distinction between `GPE` and `LOC` depends on the model's annotation scheme.

### 3.5 DATE

Identifies dates and date-related expressions.

Examples\:

- October 26, 2026
- Monday
- 2025
- Next week

Sentence\:

> The project started in January 2025.

Entities\:

\- `January 2025` → `DATE`

A model identifies the span and category; converting a phrase such as "next week" into an exact calendar date is a separate normalization task.

### 3.6 TIME

Identifies specific times or time expressions.

Examples\:

- 9:30 AM
- noon
- midnight

Sentence\:

> The meeting starts at 9:30 AM.

Entity\:

`9:30 AM` → `TIME`

### 3.7 MONEY

Identifies monetary amounts.

Examples\:

- \$500
- NPR 10,000
- €250

Sentence\:

> The laptop costs \$1,200.

Entity\:

`$1,200` → `MONEY`

The currency symbol and amount may be included together in the entity span.

### 3.8 PERCENT

Identifies percentages.

Examples\:

- 25%
- 7.5 percent
- 100%

Sentence\:

> Revenue increased by 15%.

Entity\:

`15%` → `PERCENT`

### 3.9 CARDINAL and ORDINAL

These labels distinguish general numbers from numbers expressing rank or order.

| Label | Example | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| CARDINAL | 100 students | Quantity |
| ORDINAL | first place | Position or order |

Some models classify numeric expressions differently depending on their context.

### 3.10 PRODUCT, EVENT, and WORK\_OF\_ART

These labels identify specific products, events, or creative works in annotation schemes that support them.

Examples\:

- `iPhone 17` → PRODUCT
- `Olympic Games` → EVENT
- `Hamlet` → WORK\_OF\_ART

These labels are not universal. A general-purpose model may not support all of them, and some systems use different categories.

## 4. Understanding entity spans and labels

Before implementing NER, understand two important concepts: **entity span** and **entity label**\.

Consider\:

> I study at Tribhuvan University in Kathmandu.

The entity spans and labels could be\:

| Start text | End text | Entity span | Label |
|--------|--------|--------|--------|
| Tribhuvan | University | Tribhuvan University | ORG |
| Kathmandu | Kathmandu | Kathmandu | GPE |

The entity span is the exact section of text recognized as an entity. The label identifies its category.

In practical NLP libraries, an entity is typically represented by its text, start position, end position, and label.

For example\:

```python
{
    "text": "Tribhuvan University",
    "label": "ORG",
    "start": 12,
    "end": 32
}
```

The numerical offsets above are illustrative; actual offsets must be calculated from the original string. The `start` index is usually inclusive and the `end` index exclusive.

### Why do entity spans matter?

Suppose the sentence is\:

> University students visited Kathmandu.

The word `University` is not necessarily an organization name here. A good NER system must identify the relevant span and its category using context rather than blindly labeling every capitalized word.

Also, a person entity may contain multiple tokens\:

> Marie Curie

NER should generally identify the full name as one entity, not treat `Marie` and `Curie` as unrelated people.

## 5. NER versus POS tagging

You have already studied POS tagging. Although both tasks assign labels to text, they answer different questions.

| Feature | POS tagging | NER |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Main question | What grammatical role does this token play? | Does this text refer to an entity, and what type is it? |
| Labels | NOUN, VERB, ADJ, etc. | PERSON, ORG, GPE, DATE, etc. |
| Typical unit | Individual token | One or more tokens forming an entity |
| Example | `Google` → PROPN | `Google` → ORG |
| Main purpose | Grammatical analysis | Entity extraction |

For example\:

> Google announced a product.

- POS tagging: `Google` is a proper noun.
- NER: `Google` is an organization.

A proper noun is not automatically a named entity. The word `Monday` may be a proper noun under certain tagging conventions, while NER identifies it as a date-related entity.

## 6. How does an NER system work?

At a conceptual level, a general-purpose NER pipeline follows these steps\:

**Input text**

Sundar Pichai works at Google.

**Tokenization**

Sundar &#124; Pichai &#124; works &#124; at &#124; Google &#124; .

**Contextual analysis**

Analyze word forms, surrounding words, and learned language patterns.

**Entity detection and classification**

Identify entity boundaries and assign categories.

**Structured output**

Sundar Pichai → PERSON

Google → ORG

The underlying model learns from examples of text annotated with entity spans and labels. At inference time, it predicts entities for previously unseen text.

The diagram describes the general process, not a requirement that every implementation uses exactly these separate components.

### Why context is necessary

Consider these sentences\:

- "Apple released a new phone."
- "She ate an apple."

The word `Apple` in the first sentence likely refers to a company, while `apple` in the second refers to a fruit.

An NER system must use context to distinguish the two. Some models may label the first occurrence as `ORG` and leave the second untagged.

Similarly\:

- "Amazon announced a new service." → likely an organization.
- "The village is near the Amazon." → potentially a geographical reference, depending on the sentence and annotation scheme.

Capitalization helps, but it is not sufficient by itself.

## 7. Implementing NER using spaCy

For practical NLP projects, spaCy is a good starting point because it provides pretrained NER models and a straightforward API.

### 7.1 Install spaCy and its English model

Run the following commands in your terminal\:

```bash
pip install spacy
python -m spacy download en_core_web_sm
```

The English model is downloaded separately from the spaCy Python package.

### 7.2 Basic NER implementation

```python
import spacy

# Load a pretrained English NLP model
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

text = """
Sundar Pichai works at Google.
Google is based in California.
The company announced the news in 2025.
"""

# Process the text
doc = nlp(text)

# Extract named entities
for ent in doc.ents:
    print(ent.text, ent.label_)
```

Possible output\:

```text
Sundar Pichai PERSON
Google ORG
California GPE
2025 DATE
```

Exact results can vary depending on the model version, text, and context.

Let's understand each part\:

- `spacy.load()` loads the pretrained NLP pipeline.
- `nlp(text)` processes the text.
- `doc.ents` returns the detected entity spans.
- `ent.text` gives the original text of an entity.
- `ent.label_` gives its human-readable entity category.

Unlike POS tagging, where you usually inspect each token, basic NER extraction commonly iterates over the entity spans directly.

### 7.3 Extract entity labels and character positions

You may need the start and end positions to highlight entities in a document or link them to the original text.

```python
import spacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

text = "Marie Curie was born in Warsaw in 1867."
doc = nlp(text)

for ent in doc.ents:
    print({
        "text": ent.text,
        "label": ent.label_,
        "start": ent.start_char,
        "end": ent.end_char
    })
```

Example output\:

```python
{
    "text": "Marie Curie",
    "label": "PERSON",
    "start": 0,
    "end": 11
}
```

Other detected entities may include `Warsaw` and `1867`\.

The offsets refer to character positions in the original input string. This is useful when you need to map model predictions back to the source document.

### 7.4 Understand the `Entity` object

For each `ent` in `doc.ents`, you can inspect\:

| Attribute | Purpose |
|--------|--------|
| `ent.text` | Full entity text |
| `ent.label_` | Entity category |
| `ent.start` | Start token index |
| `ent.end` | End token index, exclusive |
| `ent.start_char` | Start character offset |
| `ent.end_char` | End character offset, exclusive |
| `ent.kb_id_` | Entity knowledge-base ID, if assigned |

An important distinction\:

- `ent.start` and `ent.end` refer to token positions.
- `ent.start_char` and `ent.end_char` refer to character positions.

For text highlighting, character offsets are often more convenient.

### 7.5 Visualize NER output

spaCy provides a built-in entity visualization tool.

```python
import spacy
from spacy import displacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

text = """
Sundar Pichai works at Google.
The company is based in California.
"""

doc = nlp(text)

displacy.render(
    doc,
    style="ent",
    jupyter=True
)
```

In a Jupyter notebook, this renders the text with entity spans highlighted and labeled.

If you are running a regular Python script or want to open the visualization in a browser, you can use\:

```python
displacy.serve(doc, style="ent")
```

This starts a local visualization server. Stop it when you finish inspecting the output.

## 8. Extract only specific entity categories

Sometimes you don't want every entity. For example, a news-analysis project might need only people and organizations.

You can filter entities by label.

```python
import spacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

text = """
Sundar Pichai works at Google.
Google announced a project in California in 2025.
"""

doc = nlp(text)

for ent in doc.ents:
    if ent.label_ in {"PERSON", "ORG"}:
        print(ent.text, ent.label_)
```

Example output\:

```text
Sundar Pichai PERSON
Google ORG
Google ORG
```

Filtering does not change the model's predictions. It simply selects which detected entities you want to retain.

### Build a structured list

A list of dictionaries is convenient when you want to save the results or pass them to another function.

```python
entities = [
    {
        "text": ent.text,
        "label": ent.label_,
        "start": ent.start_char,
        "end": ent.end_char
    }
    for ent in doc.ents
]

print(entities)
```

This structure can be converted into JSON or a DataFrame, or inserted into a database.

## 9. NER on a Pandas DataFrame

Suppose you have a dataset of news articles, movie descriptions, customer messages, or research abstracts. You can apply NER to every row and store the results in a new column.

### 9.1 Example dataset

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "text": [
        "Google opened an office in Nepal in 2025.",
        "Marie Curie studied in Paris.",
        "Microsoft announced a new product."
    ]
})

print(df)
```

### 9.2 Extract entities from each row

```python
import spacy
import pandas as pd

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

def extract_entities(text):
    doc = nlp(str(text))

    return [
        {
            "text": ent.text,
            "label": ent.label_
        }
        for ent in doc.ents
    ]

df["entities"] = df["text"].apply(extract_entities)

print(df[["text", "entities"]])
```

A row might contain\:

```python
[
    {"text": "Google", "label": "ORG"},
    {"text": "Nepal", "label": "GPE"},
    {"text": "2025", "label": "DATE"}
]
```

The actual entities depend on the model's predictions.

### 9.3 Process large datasets efficiently

For larger datasets, `nlp.pipe()` is generally preferable to repeatedly calling `nlp()` for individual rows.

```python
texts = df["text"].fillna("").astype(str)

df["entities"] = [
    [
        {
            "text": ent.text,
            "label": ent.label_
        }
        for ent in doc.ents
    ]
    for doc in nlp.pipe(texts, batch_size=128)
]
```

This processes the documents in batches and reduces Python-level overhead.

If you have a very large corpus, also consider\:

- Processing only the text columns needed for NER.
- Choosing an appropriate batch size.
- Disabling pipeline components that are not required, while keeping the components needed for NER.
- Saving intermediate results so you don't need to process the same documents repeatedly.

### 9.4 Convert extracted entities into separate rows

A single document can contain multiple entities. If you want to analyze entities across a dataset, a normalized table is often more useful than a list stored inside each cell.

```python
records = []

for doc_id, doc in enumerate(nlp.pipe(texts, batch_size=128)):
    for ent in doc.ents:
        records.append({
            "doc_id": doc_id,
            "entity": ent.text,
            "label": ent.label_,
            "start_char": ent.start_char,
            "end_char": ent.end_char
        })

entities_df = pd.DataFrame(
    records,
    columns=[
        "doc_id",
        "entity",
        "label",
        "start_char",
        "end_char"
    ]
)

print(entities_df)
```

The resulting table has one row per detected entity, which makes it easier to\:

- Count organizations across articles.
- Find documents mentioning a particular person.
- Group entities by category.
- Export entities into a database or CSV file.

## 10. NER using NLTK

NLTK is useful for understanding classical NLP pipelines. It provides a named entity chunker through its standard NLP interfaces, although spaCy is often more convenient for production-style entity extraction.

### 10.1 Installation and setup

```bash
pip install nltk
```

Then download the resources required by your NLTK version\:

```python
import nltk

nltk.download("punkt_tab")
nltk.download("averaged_perceptron_tagger_eng")
nltk.download("maxent_ne_chunker_tab")
nltk.download("words")
```

Resource names can vary across NLTK versions.

### 10.2 Basic NLTK implementation

```python
import nltk

text = "Sundar Pichai works at Google in California."

tokens = nltk.word_tokenize(text)

# Assign part-of-speech tags
tagged_tokens = nltk.pos_tag(tokens)

# Identify named entity chunks
tree = nltk.ne_chunk(tagged_tokens)

print(tree)
```

The result is a tree structure containing individual tokens and recognized entity chunks.

You can inspect the chunks like this\:

```python
for subtree in tree:
    if hasattr(subtree, "label"):
        entity_text = " ".join(
            word for word, tag in subtree.leaves()
        )

        print(entity_text, subtree.label())
```

The entity categories and results can differ from spaCy's output because the two tools use different pretrained resources and annotation conventions.

### 10.3 NLTK versus spaCy

| Feature | NLTK | spaCy |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Main strength | Learning and experimenting with NLP concepts | Practical integrated NLP pipelines |
| POS tagging | Yes | Yes |
| NER | Yes, through its chunker | Yes, with pretrained pipelines |
| Lemmatization | Yes, with lexical resources | Yes |
| Entity visualization | Requires additional work | Built-in `displacy` |
| Efficient batch processing | Available through custom workflows | `nlp.pipe()` |
| Customization | Depends on the component | Supports configurable and trainable pipelines |

For your practical projects, spaCy is a good default. NLTK remains useful for learning how traditional NLP tools are organized and for experimenting with individual components.

## 11. NER using a pretrained Transformer model

Pretrained Transformer models are another option for entity extraction. The Hugging Face Transformers library provides pipelines that let you use compatible pretrained NER models without implementing the underlying neural network yourself.

### 11.1 Install the library

```bash
pip install transformers torch
```

A compatible PyTorch installation is required. Depending on your machine, GPU setup, and operating system, you may need to install a suitable PyTorch build separately.

### 11.2 Run a pretrained NER pipeline

```python
from transformers import pipeline

ner = pipeline(
    task="ner",
    model="dslim/bert-base-NER",
    aggregation_strategy="simple"
)

text = """
Sundar Pichai works at Google.
He visited New York in 2025.
"""

entities = ner(text)

for entity in entities:
    print({
        "text": entity["word"],
        "label": entity["entity_group"],
        "score": entity["score"],
        "start": entity["start"],
        "end": entity["end"]
    })
```

This model is an example of a pretrained English NER model. Its supported entity categories and output conventions differ from spaCy's.

Important fields\:

- `word`\: The extracted text span.
- `entity_group`\: The aggregated entity category.
- `score`\: The model's confidence-like prediction score.
- `start` and `end`\: Character offsets in the input text.

The score is not automatically a calibrated probability that the entity is correct.

### 11.3 When should you use spaCy or Transformers?

Use spaCy when you need a convenient general-purpose NLP pipeline with tokenization, lemmatization, POS tagging, NER, and dependency parsing.

Consider Transformers when you want to experiment with particular pretrained models, compare model performance, or adapt a model to a specialized domain.

Both can be useful. A Transformer model is not guaranteed to outperform every spaCy model on every dataset.

## 12. Custom NER: Training a model for your own entities

Pretrained models recognize categories they were designed or trained to identify. In a specialized application, you may need additional entity categories.

For example, imagine building a movie-information extraction system. You may want to extract\:

- `MOVIE_TITLE`
- `ACTOR`
- `DIRECTOR`
- `GENRE`
- `PRODUCTION_COMPANY`

A general-purpose NER model may recognize an actor's name as `PERSON`, but it will not necessarily distinguish actors from directors or identify every movie title.

You can create a custom annotated dataset and train an NER component for your target categories.

### 12.1 Create annotated training examples

Conceptually, the training data needs raw text plus the character spans and labels of the entities.

```python
TRAIN_DATA = [
    (
        "Inception was directed by Christopher Nolan.",
        {
            "entities": [
                (0, 8, "MOVIE_TITLE"),
                (24, 41, "DIRECTOR")
            ]
        }
    ),
    (
        "Tom Hanks starred in Forrest Gump.",
        {
            "entities": [
                (0, 9, "ACTOR"),
                (20, 32, "MOVIE_TITLE")
            ]
        }
    )
]
```

These are illustrative examples; in a real dataset, the offsets must exactly match the character positions in each sentence. Incorrect offsets will cause annotation errors.

### 12.2 Important steps in custom training

1. Define your entity categories and annotation guidelines.
2. Collect representative examples from the domain.
3. Annotate entity spans carefully and consistently.
4. Split the data into training, validation, and test sets.
5. Train an NER component using a compatible library and model configuration.
6. Evaluate the trained model on unseen data.
7. Inspect false positives, missed entities, and incorrect boundaries.
8. Deploy the model and monitor its performance on real inputs.

Custom NER training usually requires more than adding a few examples. A model needs enough representative examples to learn the distinctions in your domain, and the amount of data required depends on the task's complexity.

You should also keep the train/test split at the document level when multiple highly similar sentences come from the same document, to avoid overly optimistic evaluation.

## 13. Evaluating an NER model

An NER model can make three broad types of errors\:

- **False positive\:** It labels text as an entity when it should not.
- **False negative\:** It misses an entity that should have been identified.
- **Incorrect classification\:** It detects an entity but assigns the wrong category.

Entity boundaries can also be wrong. For example, the correct entity may be `Tribhuvan University`, but a model might extract only `Tribhuvan`\.

### 13.1 Precision

Precision measures how many predicted entities are correct.

$$
\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}
$$

Where\:

- $TP$ \= correctly predicted entities.
- $FP$ \= incorrectly predicted entities.

High precision means the model makes relatively few false-positive predictions.

### 13.2 Recall

Recall measures how many of the actual entities the model successfully finds.

$$
\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
$$

Where $FN$ represents actual entities the model missed.

High recall means the model misses relatively few entities.

### 13.3 F1-score

The F1-score combines precision and recall using their harmonic mean.

$$
F_1=2\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}
{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}
$$

For example, suppose an NER model has\:

- 80 correctly detected entities.
- 20 false positives.
- 20 missed entities.

Then\:

$$
\text{Precision}=\frac{80}{80+20}=0.8
$$

$$
\text{Recall}=\frac{80}{80+20}=0.8
$$

$$
F_1=0.8
$$

Therefore, precision, recall, and F1-score are all 80% in this example.

### 13.4 Entity-level versus token-level evaluation

For NER, **exact entity-level evaluation** is especially important.

Suppose the actual entity is\:

> Tribhuvan University

But the model predicts\:

> Tribhuvan

Even if the model identifies the correct beginning of the entity, its boundary is incorrect. Under strict entity-level evaluation, the prediction does not count as an exact match.

Evaluation also considers the entity category. Predicting the correct span but labeling it `PERSON` instead of `ORG` is an error.

When comparing NER models, make sure they use the same annotation scheme and evaluation rules.

## 14. Common challenges and limitations of NER

### 14.1 Ambiguous entity names

A word can represent different things depending on context.

- `Apple` can refer to a company or a fruit.
- `Jordan` can refer to a person or a country.
- `Amazon` can refer to a company or a geographical region.

Context helps, but models can still make mistakes.

### 14.2 Unseen entities

A general-purpose model may not recognize an unfamiliar startup, newly released product, or local institution.

This is especially common when the entity was absent from the training data or appears in an unfamiliar context.

### 14.3 Nested entities

Consider\:

> University of California, Berkeley

This expression contains a larger organization name and a geographical name within it.

Different annotation schemes may permit overlapping or nested entity spans, while many standard NER pipelines return non-overlapping entities. A system's output format may therefore affect what can be represented.

### 14.4 Domain-specific language

A general-purpose model may struggle with technical terminology.

For example, medical NER requires specialized categories for diseases, chemicals, medications, and procedures. Legal and financial documents have their own entity types and conventions.

### 14.5 Multilingual text

English NER models should not be assumed to work well for Nepali or other languages.

For Nepali-language applications, look for a model trained or evaluated on Nepali text, verify its supported entity categories, and test it against manually annotated examples from your target domain.

### 14.6 Entity normalization and linking

NER identifies a text span and its category, but it does not necessarily determine exactly which real-world entity the text refers to.

For example, `Kathmandu` might be extracted as a location. Connecting that mention to a unique database record or knowledge-base identifier is a separate task called **entity linking**\.

Likewise, turning the phrase `next Monday` into a standardized calendar date requires date normalization rather than NER alone.

## 15. NER versus related NLP tasks

| Task | Purpose | Example |
|--------|--------|--------|
| POS tagging | Identify grammatical categories | `Google` → PROPN |
| Lemmatization | Find dictionary base forms | `running` → `run` |
| NER | Identify named entities | `Google` → ORG |
| Entity linking | Link a mention to a specific real-world entity | Link `Google` to a company record |
| Relation extraction | Identify relationships between entities | `Person` — works at → `Company` |
| Sentiment analysis | Predict sentiment or opinion | Review → positive |
| Information extraction | Broader task of extracting structured facts | Extract entities, relations, dates, and events |

These tasks often work together.

For example, a system analyzing news articles may use NER to find companies and people, relation extraction to identify who works for which company, and entity linking to connect names to database records.

## 16. Mini-project: Build a news entity extractor

Let's put the concepts together in a small practical project using spaCy.

### Project goal

Given several news snippets, extract entities, categorize them, and store the results in a CSV file.

### Complete implementation

```python
import pandas as pd
import spacy

# Load the pretrained English NLP pipeline
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

# Example news dataset
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "article": [
        "Google announced a new initiative in Nepal in 2025.",
        "Microsoft opened an office in London.",
        "Marie Curie studied in Paris.",
        "The meeting will take place on October 26, 2026."
    ]
})

# Handle missing values
df["article"] = df["article"].fillna("").astype(str)

# Extract entities efficiently
entity_records = []

for doc_id, doc in enumerate(
    nlp.pipe(df["article"], batch_size=128)
):
    for ent in doc.ents:
        entity_records.append({
            "article_id": doc_id,
            "entity": ent.text,
            "label": ent.label_,
            "start_char": ent.start_char,
            "end_char": ent.end_char
        })

# Convert extracted entities to a DataFrame
entities_df = pd.DataFrame(
    entity_records,
    columns=[
        "article_id",
        "entity",
        "label",
        "start_char",
        "end_char"
    ]
)

# Save the extracted entities
entities_df.to_csv("news_entities.csv", index=False)

print(entities_df)
```

## 18. Final revision notes

Remember these concepts\:

- **NER\:** Finds named entities and classifies them into categories.
- **Entity span\:** The exact text corresponding to an entity.
- **Entity label\:** The category assigned to the span.
- **spaCy\:** A convenient choice for pretrained NER, entity visualization, and batch processing.
- **NLTK\:** Useful for learning traditional NLP and experimenting with entity chunking.
- **Transformers\:** Provide additional pretrained models and options for specialized tasks.
- **Custom NER\:** Requires annotated examples and evaluation on unseen data.
- **Evaluation\:** Precision, recall, F1-score, and exact entity-span matching are important.
- **Entity linking\:** Resolves an extracted mention to a specific real-world entity.

**Recommended practical task\:** Run the spaCy implementation on a collection of movie reviews or news articles, save the extracted entities in a DataFrame, and analyze which people, organizations, and locations occur most frequently.
