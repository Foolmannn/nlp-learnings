
# Sequence Labeling Models in NLP — Detailed Study Notes

Sequence labeling is an important NLP topic that builds on what you've learned about **tokenization, POS tagging, lemmatization, and Named Entity Recognition (NER)**\.

In POS tagging, we assign a grammatical label to each token. In NER, we identify tokens that belong to entities such as people, organizations, and locations. Sequence labeling models are approaches that learn to predict these labels while considering relationships between tokens in a sentence.

We'll study the main approaches from traditional statistical methods to modern deep learning and Transformer-based models, with Python examples and practical guidance.

## 1. What is sequence labeling?

**Sequence labeling** is an NLP task in which a model receives a sequence of tokens and predicts a label for each token.

For example, consider this sentence\:

> Sundar Pichai works at Google.

After tokenization, the input sequence is\:

### Input sequence

---

### Predicted labels (NER)

B = beginning of an entity, I = inside an entity, O = outside any entity.

The model predicts a label for each token, rather than one label for the entire sentence.

### Common sequence-labeling tasks

| Task | Example output |
|--------|--------|
| POS tagging | `works → VERB` |
| Named Entity Recognition | `Google → ORG` |
| Chunking | `the big dog → noun phrase` |
| Slot filling | `Kathmandu → destination` |
| Word-level language tasks | Assigning a category to each word or token |

Sequence labeling differs from ordinary text classification. In sentiment classification, a whole review might receive one label such as `positive`\. In sequence labeling, every token may receive its own label.

## 2. Why do we need sequence-labeling models?

A simple model might classify each word independently. But the correct label for a word often depends on surrounding words.

Consider\:

- “Apple released a new phone.”
- “I ate an apple.”

The word *Apple/apple* has different meanings and potentially different entity labels depending on context.

Likewise, consider\:

- “New York is crowded.”
- “York is a historic city.”

The model must learn where an entity begins and ends, and how the words relate to each other.

A useful conceptual objective is\:

$$
\hat{Y}=\arg\max_Y P(Y\mid X)
$$

where\:

- $X=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$ is the input token sequence.
- $Y=(y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_n)$ is the predicted label sequence.
- $\hat{Y}$ is the best predicted sequence.

The important point is that the model tries to predict a **coherent sequence of labels**, not just independent guesses.

## 3. The major sequence-labeling approaches

The main approaches you'll encounter are\:

**1\. Hidden Markov Model (HMM)**

(Traditional statistical ML)

Models relationships between hidden labels and observed words using transition and emission probabilities.

---

**2\. Viterbi algorithm**

(Decoding algorithm)

Finds the most likely sequence of labels. It is commonly used with HMMs; it is not itself a model.

---

**3\. Conditional Random Field (CRF)**

(Discriminative ML)

Predicts labels based on the input sequence and learns dependencies between neighboring labels.

---

**4\. BiLSTM**

(Deep learning)

Uses context from both the left and right sides of a token to produce contextual representations.

---

**5\. Transformer-based models**

(Modern NLP)

Models such as BERT use attention and contextual representations to predict a label for each token.

These approaches are related, but they are not all the same type of thing: HMM and CRF are probabilistic models, Viterbi is an inference algorithm, BiLSTM is a neural network architecture, and Transformer is another neural architecture.

---

## 4. Hidden Markov Models (HMM)

### 4.1 What is an HMM?

A **Hidden Markov Model** is a probabilistic model for sequences. It assumes that we observe words, but the labels or states that generated those words are hidden.

For POS tagging\:

- **Observed sequence\:** words in a sentence.
- **Hidden sequence\:** POS tags.
- **Goal\:** find the most likely tag for every word.

Example\:

| Observed word | Hidden POS tag |
|--------|--------|
| The | DET |
| cat | NOUN |
| sleeps | VERB |

The words are observed, but the model must infer their tags.

### 4.2 The two core probabilities

An HMM commonly uses two types of probabilities.

**A. Transition probability**

The probability of a tag given the previous tag\:

$$
P(y_t\mid y_{t-1})
$$

For example\:

$$
P(\text{NOUN}\mid\text{DET})
$$

This represents how likely a noun is to follow a determiner.

**B. Emission probability**

The probability of a word given its tag\:

$$
P(x_t\mid y_t)
$$

For example\:

$$
P(\text{cat}\mid\text{NOUN})
$$

This represents how likely the word *cat* is to be emitted by the NOUN state.

An HMM also typically has an initial-state probability $P(y_1)$\.

### 4.3 The Markov assumption

A basic HMM assumes the current tag depends only on the previous tag\:

$$
P(y_t\mid y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1})
\approx P(y_t\mid y_{t-1})
$$

This simplifies computation. The model doesn't directly condition the current tag on the entire history of previous tags.

It also makes simplifying assumptions about observations, including that a word is conditionally independent of other words given its tag.

These assumptions are useful, but natural language is more complicated than this.

### 4.4 Calculating a sequence probability

For a sequence of words $X$ and tags $Y$, the HMM score is\:

$$
P(X,Y)=P(y_1)P(x_1\mid y_1)
\prod_{t=2}^{n}P(y_t\mid y_{t-1})P(x_t\mid y_t)
$$

The model evaluates possible tag sequences and seeks the most likely one\:

$$
\hat{Y}=\arg\max_Y P(X,Y)
$$

For instance, suppose the sentence is “dogs bark.”

The model compares candidate sequences such as\:

- `NOUN VERB`
- `VERB NOUN`
- `NOUN NOUN`

A high probability for `NOUN → VERB`, combined with high emission probabilities for *dogs* as a noun and *bark* as a verb, makes `NOUN VERB` a strong candidate.

In real implementations, calculations are usually performed in log space to prevent numerical underflow when multiplying many small probabilities.

### 4.5 Training an HMM

If a labeled corpus is available, probabilities can be estimated by counting occurrences.

Transition probability using maximum-likelihood estimation\:

$$
P(y_t\mid y_{t-1})
=\frac{C(y_{t-1},y_t)}{C(y_{t-1})}
$$

Emission probability\:

$$
P(x\mid y)=\frac{C(y,x)}{C(y)}
$$

Here, $C(a,b)$ is the count of the pair, and $C(a)$ is the count of the conditioning event.

For example, if `DET → NOUN` occurs 80 times out of 100 transitions from `DET`, the estimated transition probability is $0.8$\.

**Problem: unseen words and transitions.** If a word or tag transition never appeared in training, its probability might be zero. Smoothing methods, such as add-one or more sophisticated smoothing, can reduce this problem. Unknown words remain a challenge.

### 4.6 Implementing an HMM tagger in Python

For a practical implementation, NLTK provides an HMM tagger. This example trains a tagger on a small, tagged corpus from NLTK.

```python
import nltk
from nltk.corpus import treebank
from nltk.tag import hmm

nltk.download("treebank")

# The Penn Treebank corpus contains words paired with POS tags.
tagged_sentences = treebank.tagged_sents()

# Split into training and testing data.
split_index = int(len(tagged_sentences) * 0.8)
train_data = tagged_sentences[:split_index]
test_data = tagged_sentences[split_index:]

# Train the HMM.
trainer = hmm.HiddenMarkovModelTrainer()
tagger = trainer.train_supervised(train_data)

# Predict POS tags for a new sentence.
words = ["The", "movie", "was", "excellent", "."]
predicted_tags = tagger.tag(words)

print(predicted_tags)
```

The exact predictions depend on the training data and its vocabulary. Since this is a basic HMM, it may struggle with words it hasn't seen before.

To evaluate the tagger\:

```python
accuracy = tagger.accuracy(test_data)
print("Test accuracy:", accuracy)
```

This estimates token-level tagging accuracy on the test sentences. For a more reliable evaluation, ensure that the train/test split is representative and that preprocessing matches between training and inference.

### 4.7 Advantages and limitations of HMMs

**Advantages**

- Probabilistic and relatively easy to explain.
- Efficient for modest sequence sizes.
- Provides a clear foundation for sequence modeling.
- Useful for understanding transition and emission probabilities.

**Limitations**

- Strong independence assumptions.
- Limited ability to model long-range context.
- Unknown words can be difficult to handle.
- Often outperformed by modern contextual neural models on complex NLP tasks.

## 5. Viterbi algorithm

### 5.1 What does Viterbi do?

Even with an HMM, the number of possible label sequences grows exponentially with sentence length.

If a sentence has $n$ tokens and each token can have $k$ labels, there are $k^n$ possible sequences.

The **Viterbi algorithm** uses dynamic programming to find the best sequence without enumerating every possible sequence.

It is often used to decode HMMs. Similar dynamic-programming ideas can be applied to other structured prediction models, such as linear-chain CRFs.

### 5.2 The central idea

Instead of remembering every possible sequence, Viterbi stores the best score for each possible current tag at each position.

Define\:

$$
V_t(s)=\max_{y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1}}
P(y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1},y_t=s,x_1,\ldots,x_t)
$$

In words, $V_t(s)$ is the best score for any partial path that ends with state $s$ at position $t$\.

The recurrence is\:

$$
V_t(s)=P(x_t\mid s)\max_{s'}
\left[V_{t-1}(s')P(s\mid s')\right]
$$

Here\:

- $s$ is the current tag.
- $s'$ is a possible previous tag.
- $P(s\mid s')$ is the transition probability.
- $P(x_t\mid s)$ is the emission probability.

The algorithm stores the best preceding state for each current state, then traces those pointers backward to reconstruct the best complete sequence.

### 5.3 A small numerical example

Suppose the two possible tags for a word are `NOUN` and `VERB`\.

At the next position, assume the previous tag scores are\:

- `NOUN`\: $0.40$
- `VERB`\: $0.30$

For a candidate current `VERB`, suppose\:

- $P(\text{VERB}\mid\text{NOUN})=0.6$
- $P(\text{VERB}\mid\text{VERB})=0.2$
- The current word's emission probability under `VERB` is $0.5$\.

Then\:

$$
V_t(\text{VERB})=
0.5\max(0.40\times0.6,\;0.30\times0.2)
$$

$$
=0.5\max(0.24,\;0.06)=0.12
$$

So the best path ending in `VERB` comes from the preceding `NOUN` state.

This computation is repeated for every current tag at every position.

### 5.4 Viterbi complexity

For a sequence of $n$ tokens and $k$ states, a basic dense implementation takes approximately\:

- **Time\:** $O(nk^2)$
- **Space\:** $O(nk)$ when storing all scores and backpointers for reconstruction.

With structured or sparse transitions, computation can be lower.

### 5.5 Viterbi versus greedy decoding

A greedy decoder chooses the best tag at each position based on the current local decision. It can make an early choice that causes a poor overall sequence.

Viterbi considers all possible previous states when calculating each current state's best score. This allows it to find the globally best sequence under the model's scoring assumptions.

**Remember\:** Viterbi doesn't learn probabilities by itself. It finds the best path using probabilities or scores supplied by a model.

## 6. Conditional Random Fields (CRF)

### 6.1 What is a CRF?

A **Conditional Random Field (CRF)** is a probabilistic model that predicts a sequence of labels directly from an observed input sequence.

Unlike a basic HMM, which models the joint probability of words and labels, a CRF models the conditional probability\:

$$
P(Y\mid X)
$$

That means it asks\:

> Given the words in this sentence, which sequence of labels is most likely?

CRFs are particularly useful when the input contains many features that help determine labels.

### 6.2 HMM vs. CRF

| Feature | HMM | CRF |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Probability modeled | $P(X,Y)$ | $P(Y\mid X)$ |
| Model type | Generative | Discriminative |
| Input features | Often modeled through emissions | Can use rich features of the input |
| Label dependencies | Usually adjacent states | Can explicitly score neighboring labels |
| Inference | Often Viterbi | Often Viterbi for linear-chain CRFs |
| Typical limitation | Strong observation assumptions | Training and inference can be more computationally demanding |

A CRF can use features of the current word, capitalization, suffixes, neighboring words, and other observations without requiring a full generative model of how the words were produced.

### 6.3 Linear-chain CRF

The most common CRF for token labeling is the **linear-chain CRF**\.

It scores a complete label sequence by combining two types of scores\:

1. **Emission or state scores\:** how suitable a label is for the current input token.
2. **Transition scores\:** how suitable one label is after another label.

A common formulation is\:

$$
P(Y\mid X)=
\frac{\exp(S(X,Y))}{Z(X)}
$$

where\:

- $S(X,Y)$ is the score of the entire label sequence.
- $Z(X)$ is a normalization term that sums scores across all possible label sequences.

The sequence score can be expressed as\:

$$
S(X,Y)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}
\left[A_{y_{t-1},y_t}+E_{t,y_t}\right]
$$

where\:

- $A_{y_{t-1},y_t}$ is the transition score.
- $E_{t,y_t}$ is the input-dependent score for assigning label $y_t$ at position $t$\.

The transition score can discourage implausible label combinations. For example, under a BIO tagging scheme, a sequence that begins with `I-PER` may be penalized or disallowed, depending on the model's constraints and training setup.

### 6.4 How is a CRF trained?

Training generally involves increasing the probability of the correct label sequence relative to competing sequences.

For a training dataset, the negative log-likelihood objective is\:

$$
\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P(Y^{(i)}\mid X^{(i)})
$$

The model learns feature and transition weights that make the correct label sequences score highly.

At prediction time, a decoding algorithm such as Viterbi finds the highest-scoring label sequence.

### 6.5 Implementing a CRF in Python

One common Python package is `sklearn-crfsuite`\. This example trains a CRF for POS tagging on NLTK's Penn Treebank corpus.

Install the packages\:

```bash
pip install sklearn-crfsuite nltk
```

Code\:

```python
import nltk
import sklearn_crfsuite
from nltk.corpus import treebank
from sklearn_crfsuite import metrics

nltk.download("treebank")

tagged_sentences = treebank.tagged_sents()

split = int(len(tagged_sentences) * 0.8)
train_data = tagged_sentences[:split]
test_data = tagged_sentences[split:]
```

First, create features for each token\:

```python
def word_features(sentence, i):
    word = sentence[i][0]

    features = {
        "bias": 1.0,
        "word.lower": word.lower(),
        "word[-3:]": word[-3:],
        "word[-2:]": word[-2:],
        "word.isupper": word.isupper(),
        "word.istitle": word.istitle(),
        "word.isdigit": word.isdigit(),
    }

    if i > 0:
        previous = sentence[i - 1][0]
        features.update({
            "-1:word.lower": previous.lower(),
            "-1:word.istitle": previous.istitle(),
        })
    else:
        features["BOS"] = True

    if i < len(sentence) - 1:
        following = sentence[i + 1][0]
        features.update({
            "+1:word.lower": following.lower(),
            "+1:word.istitle": following.istitle(),
        })
    else:
        features["EOS"] = True

    return features

def sentence_features(sentence):
    return [
        word_features(sentence, i)
        for i in range(len(sentence))
    ]

def sentence_labels(sentence):
    return [tag for _, tag in sentence]
```

Convert the corpus into model inputs\:

```python
X_train = [sentence_features(s) for s in train_data]
y_train = [sentence_labels(s) for s in train_data]

X_test = [sentence_features(s) for s in test_data]
y_test = [sentence_labels(s) for s in test_data]
```

Train and evaluate\:

```python
crf = sklearn_crfsuite.CRF(
    algorithm="lbfgs",
    c1=0.1,
    c2=0.1,
    max_iterations=100,
    all_possible_transitions=True,
)

crf.fit(X_train, y_train)

y_pred = crf.predict(X_test)

print(
    "Token-level F1:",
    metrics.flat_f1_score(
        y_test,
        y_pred,
        average="weighted"
    )
)
```

Here, `c1` and `c2` control L1 and L2 regularization, respectively. Regularization can help reduce overfitting.

This is a relatively expensive training example because the corpus contains many sentences. For experimentation, start with a smaller subset, then scale up.

### 6.6 When are CRFs useful?

CRFs are useful when\:

- Labels have meaningful dependencies.
- You can engineer useful token-level features.
- You want a structured prediction model without building a large neural network.
- You want to combine contextual token scores with transition constraints.

A CRF can also be used as the output layer of a neural network. For example, a BiLSTM can produce contextual token representations, and a CRF can select the best label sequence from those representations.

## 7. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and LSTMs

Before studying BiLSTM, it helps to understand recurrent neural networks.

### 7.1 What is an RNN?

An RNN processes a sequence one element at a time and maintains a hidden state that carries information from earlier elements.

At time step $t$\:

$$
h_t=f(W_xx_t+W_hh_{t-1}+b)
$$

where\:

- $x_t$ is the representation of the current token.
- $h_{t-1}$ is the previous hidden state.
- $h_t$ is the updated hidden state.
- $W_x,W_h,b$ are learned parameters.
- $f$ is a nonlinear activation function.

For example, when reading “The movie was not good,” the hidden state at *good* may contain information about the earlier word *not*\.

A basic RNN can struggle to preserve information across long sequences because of vanishing and exploding gradients during training.

### 7.2 What is an LSTM?

A **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)** network is a type of RNN designed to preserve useful information over longer sequences.

It uses a cell state and gates that regulate information flow.

The main components are\:

- **Forget gate\:** decides what information to remove.
- **Input gate\:** decides what new information to store.
- **Cell state\:** carries information through the sequence.
- **Output gate\:** controls what information is exposed as the hidden state.

The cell state is updated using learned gate values\:

$$
c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde{c}_t
$$

where\:

- $f_t$ is the forget gate.
- $i_t$ is the input gate.
- $\tilde{c}_t$ is the candidate cell content.
- $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

The gates are learned from training data. This lets the network decide which information should be retained or updated.

### 7.3 Why not use a simple RNN?

A simple RNN repeatedly combines its current input and previous hidden state. As a sequence gets longer, it can become difficult to learn dependencies between distant tokens.

LSTMs use gated memory to make this easier, although they do not completely eliminate the difficulty.

LSTMs process tokens sequentially, so they are also less naturally parallelizable across the sequence than Transformer encoders.

## 8. Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM)

### 8.1 What is a BiLSTM?

A **Bidirectional LSTM** uses two LSTMs\:

- A forward LSTM processes the sequence from left to right.
- A backward LSTM processes the sequence from right to left.

Their hidden states are combined to create a representation of each token.

$$
\overrightarrow{h_t}=\operatorname{LSTM}_{f}(x_t,\overrightarrow{h}_{t-1})
$$

$$
\overleftarrow{h_t}=\operatorname{LSTM}_{b}(x_t,\overleftarrow{h}_{t+1})
$$

The combined representation is often\:

$$
h_t=[\overrightarrow{h_t};\overleftarrow{h_t}]
$$

The semicolon means concatenation.

### 8.2 Why is bidirectional context important?

Consider the sentence\:

> I visited Jordan last summer.

The word *Jordan* might refer to a person or a country. The surrounding words help resolve its meaning.

A forward-only model sees only the preceding context when it reaches *Jordan*\. A BiLSTM can also incorporate the words that follow it.

This makes bidirectional architectures useful for tasks where the full sentence is available before prediction.

A standard bidirectional model is not inherently suitable for strict real-time prediction where future tokens have not arrived yet.

### 8.3 BiLSTM for NER

A common NER architecture works as follows\:

**Input tokens**

Sundar · Pichai · works · at · Google

**Word embeddings**

Dense vector for each token

Forward LSTM

Left → right

Backward LSTM

Right → left

**Concatenated contextual vectors**

**Token-level classification layer**

B-PER · I-PER · O · O · B-ORG

The final classification layer produces a score for each possible label at each token. The predicted labels can be selected independently, or a CRF can be added to improve sequence consistency.

### 8.4 Implementing a BiLSTM tagger in PyTorch

The following is a compact educational model for POS tagging. It assumes you already have tokenized sentences and aligned POS tags in a training dataset.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class BiLSTMTagger(nn.Module):
    def __init__(
        self,
        vocab_size,
        num_tags,
        embedding_dim=100,
        hidden_dim=128,
        padding_idx=0
    ):
        super().__init__()

        self.embedding = nn.Embedding(
            vocab_size,
            embedding_dim,
            padding_idx=padding_idx
        )

        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(
            input_size=embedding_dim,
            hidden_size=hidden_dim,
            batch_first=True,
            bidirectional=True
        )

        self.classifier = nn.Linear(
            hidden_dim * 2,
            num_tags
        )

    def forward(self, input_ids):
        # [batch, sequence_length, embedding_dim]
        embedded = self.embedding(input_ids)

        # [batch, sequence_length, hidden_dim * 2]
        outputs, _ = self.lstm(embedded)

        # [batch, sequence_length, num_tags]
        logits = self.classifier(outputs)

        return logits
```

Example model initialization\:

```python
vocab_size = 10000
num_tags = 17

model = BiLSTMTagger(
    vocab_size=vocab_size,
    num_tags=num_tags
)

# A batch of two padded sentences.
input_ids = torch.tensor([
    [2, 14, 7, 5, 0],
    [2, 31, 18, 9, 6]
])

logits = model(input_ids)

print(logits.shape)
# torch.Size([2, 5, 17])
```

The output shape is\:

`(batch size, sequence length, number of labels)`

Each token has one score for each possible tag.

**Important\:** This defines the architecture, not a complete trained tagger. To train it correctly, you also need\:

1. A word-to-ID vocabulary and tag-to-ID mapping.
2. Aligned input and target sequences.
3. Padding and a mask so padded tokens don't contribute to the loss.
4. A loss function such as cross-entropy.
5. An optimizer such as Adam.
6. A training loop and held-out evaluation data.

For example, if `labels` has shape `[batch, sequence_length]`, you can flatten the token dimensions for cross-entropy. Use an ignore index for padded target positions\:

```python
criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(ignore_index=-100)

logits = model(input_ids)

# labels should use -100 for padding positions.
loss = criterion(
    logits.reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)),
    labels.reshape(-1)
)
```

This assumes `labels` has already been created and aligned with `input_ids`\. The model still needs a training loop to learn its parameters.

### 8.5 BiLSTM advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Learns representations from context in both directions.
- Handles variable-length sequences.
- Works well for token-level tasks with sufficient training data.
- Can be combined with a CRF output layer.

**Limitations**

- Training is sequential along each direction.
- Performance depends on data quality, embeddings, and model size.
- Can struggle with very long-range relationships.
- Usually requires more task-specific training than using a strong pretrained language model.

## 9. Transformer-based sequence labeling

Transformers are central to modern NLP because they create contextual representations using **self-attention**\.

Models such as BERT can be fine-tuned for POS tagging, NER, and other token-level classification tasks.

### 9.1 What is self-attention?

Self-attention allows each token to assign different levels of importance to other tokens in the input.

Consider\:

> The animal didn't cross the road because it was tired.

To interpret *it*, the model can use information from other words in the sentence. Self-attention provides a mechanism for combining information from across the input, rather than passing it only through a recurrent hidden state.

Each token is transformed into three vectors\:

- **Query (Q)\:** what the token is looking for.
- **Key (K)\:** what information the token offers.
- **Value (V)\:** the information to combine.

The attention calculation is\:

$$
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

Here, $d_k$ is the dimension of the key vectors. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ helps control the magnitude of the attention scores.

The model learns the query, key, and value transformations during training.

### 9.2 How a Transformer labels tokens

A token-classification model generally follows this structure\:

**Text**

Sundar Pichai works at Google

**Tokenizer**

Subword tokens and token IDs

**Pretrained Transformer encoder**

Contextual representations from self-attention

**Token classification head**

Scores for each possible label

**Predicted label sequence**

B-PER · I-PER · O · O · B-ORG

A typical token-classification head is a linear layer that maps each contextual token representation to label scores.

If the Transformer hidden dimension is $d$ and there are $K$ labels, the head usually maps\:

$$
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
$$

For each token, the model produces $K$ scores, which can be converted into probabilities using softmax.

### 9.3 Why use a pretrained model?

Training a Transformer from scratch requires a large dataset and substantial computation. A pretrained model has already learned useful language representations from large-scale text.

Fine-tuning adapts those representations to a specific task, such as NER.

For example, a pretrained BERT model may already recognize patterns associated with names, places, and organizations. Fine-tuning teaches it the label scheme and conventions of your specific dataset.

### 9.4 Run a pretrained Transformer NER model

Since you've already studied NER, this is a useful way to connect the concepts to a working implementation.

Install the dependencies\:

Run the model\:

```python
from transformers import pipeline

ner = pipeline(
    task="ner",
    model="dslim/bert-base-NER",
    aggregation_strategy="simple"
)

text = (
    "Sundar Pichai works at Google. "
    "The company is based in California."
```

The output is a list of detected entity spans. Exact predictions and scores depend on the model version and runtime.

The `aggregation_strategy="simple"` setting combines suitable subword predictions into entity spans. This is convenient for inference, but it doesn't mean the model has learned every possible entity boundary perfectly.

### 9.5 Fine-tune a Transformer for token classification

If you want to train your own model for a custom labeling task, the Hugging Face Transformers library provides token-classification models.

Install the relevant packages\:

```bash
pip install transformers datasets evaluate accelerate
```

Choose a pretrained encoder and create a label mapping\:

```python
from transformers import (
    AutoTokenizer,
    AutoModelForTokenClassification
)

model_name = "bert-base-cased"

labels = [
    "O",
    "B-PER",
    "I-PER",
    "B-ORG",
    "I-ORG",
    "B-LOC",
    "I-LOC"
]

label2id = {
    label: i for i, label in enumerate(labels)
}

id2label = {
    i: label for label, i in label2id.items()
}

tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)

model = AutoModelForTokenClassification.from_pretrained(
    model_name,
    num_labels=len(labels),
    label2id=label2id,
    id2label=id2label
)
```

This initializes a token-classification model with a classification head for your labels. The model's classification head is newly initialized, so it still needs fine-tuning on labeled examples.

#### The important token-alignment problem

Transformers often tokenize words into subwords. For example, one word may become several tokens. Your dataset may label words, while the model predicts labels at the subword level.

Suppose your original example is\:

```python
tokens = ["Sundar", "Pichai", "works", "at", "Google"]
tags = ["B-PER", "I-PER", "O", "O", "B-ORG"]
```

You must decide how to align the original labels to the model's subword tokens. A common training approach assigns the original word's label to its first subword and ignores subsequent subwords in the loss. Another approach propagates labels to subwords, with appropriate adjustments to the labeling scheme.

Here is the first-subword strategy\:

```python
encoding = tokenizer(
    tokens,
    is_split_into_words=True,
    truncation=True
)

word_ids = encoding.word_ids()

aligned_labels = []
previous_word_id = None

for word_id in word_ids:
    if word_id is None:
        # Special tokens such as [CLS] and [SEP].
        aligned_labels.append(-100)

    elif word_id != previous_word_id:
        # First subword of a word.
        aligned_labels.append(
            label2id[tags[word_id]]
        )

    else:
        # Ignore additional subwords in the loss.
        aligned_labels.append(-100)

    previous_word_id = word_id

encoding["labels"] = aligned_labels
```

This snippet demonstrates alignment for one sentence. To train properly, you must apply the same logic to all examples, batch them with padding, and use a compatible data collator and training loop.

**Why use `-100`?** PyTorch's cross-entropy loss ignores targets with this index by default. It is commonly used for special tokens, padding positions, and subwords excluded from the loss.

### 9.6 Do Transformers need a CRF?

Not necessarily. A standard Transformer token classifier predicts a label distribution at each token, typically using cross-entropy during training. The most likely label may then be selected independently at each position.

A CRF can be added on top of Transformer representations to model label transitions and find a coherent sequence. Whether this improves results depends on the dataset, label scheme, architecture, and training setup.

### 9.7 Advantages and limitations of Transformers

**Advantages**

- Rich contextual representations.
- Can learn relationships across distant parts of the input.
- Pretrained models often perform well with fine-tuning.
- Widely available through Hugging Face and other libraries.

**Limitations**

- Higher memory and compute requirements than many traditional models.
- Input length is limited by the model configuration.
- Subword tokenization complicates label alignment.
- Fine-tuning needs careful evaluation and can overfit small datasets.
- Predictions can still be incorrect, especially for ambiguous or domain-specific entities.

## 10. Comparing all the approaches

| Approach | How it handles context | Typical decoding | Main strength |
|--------|--------|--------|--------|
| HMM | Previous label and current observation | Viterbi | Simple probabilistic foundation |
| Viterbi | Uses supplied sequence scores | Dynamic programming | Finds the best path efficiently |
| CRF | Input features and label transitions | Often Viterbi | Structured label prediction |
| RNN | Earlier tokens through hidden state | Often per-token prediction | Sequential context modeling |
| BiLSTM | Both left and right context | Often per-token prediction; optionally CRF | Bidirectional contextual representations |
| Transformer | Self-attention over input tokens | Often per-token prediction; optionally CRF | Strong pretrained contextual representations |

A useful distinction is that **Viterbi is not a competing model architecture**\. It is an algorithm that can decode predictions from models such as HMMs and linear-chain CRFs.

Also, a CRF and a BiLSTM are not mutually exclusive. A common architecture combines the BiLSTM's contextual representations with a CRF's sequence-level decoding.

## 11. Evaluating sequence-labeling models

A model can achieve high token accuracy while still making important entity-boundary errors. Therefore, the evaluation metric should match the task.

### 11.1 Token-level accuracy

Token accuracy measures the proportion of tokens whose predicted labels match their gold labels.

$$
\text{Accuracy}=
\frac{\text{Correctly labeled tokens}}
{\text{Total tokens}}
$$

For example, if a sentence has 100 tokens and 92 are correctly labeled, accuracy is 92%.

However, consider a dataset where 95% of tokens are labeled `O` \(outside an entity). A model that predicts `O` for every token could achieve high accuracy while failing to identify entities.

### 11.2 Precision, recall, and F1

For entity extraction, these are often more informative\:

$$
\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}
$$

$$
\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
$$

$$
F_1=2\frac{PR}{P+R}
$$

- **True positive (TP)\:** an entity was correctly predicted.
- **False positive (FP)\:** an incorrect entity was predicted.
- **False negative (FN)\:** a real entity was missed.

For NER, exact entity-level evaluation typically requires both the span boundaries and entity type to match.

Example\:

- Gold: `New York → LOC`
- Prediction: `New → LOC`

The prediction has the wrong boundary, so it is not an exact match.

### 11.3 Evaluation with Python

For a sequence-labeling dataset, you can calculate token accuracy and a classification report with scikit-learn\:

```python
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

y_true = ["B-PER", "I-PER", "O", "O", "B-ORG"]
y_pred = ["B-PER", "I-PER", "O", "B-ORG", "B-ORG"]

print("Accuracy:", accuracy_score(y_true, y_pred))

print(
    classification_report(
        y_true,
        y_pred,
        zero_division=0
    )
)
```

This calculates per-label metrics, treating each token label as a class. It does **not** calculate exact entity-level F1. For NER, libraries such as `seqeval` can evaluate complete entity spans.

```bash
pip install seqeval
```

```python
from seqeval.metrics import classification_report
from seqeval.metrics import f1_score

y_true = [[
    "B-PER", "I-PER", "O", "O", "B-ORG"
]]

y_pred = [[
    "B-PER", "I-PER", "O", "B-ORG", "B-ORG"
]]

print(classification_report(y_true, y_pred))
print("Entity-level F1:", f1_score(y_true, y_pred))
```

Use the same label scheme for the gold and predicted sequences. For rigorous experiments, evaluate on a held-out test set that was not used for training or hyperparameter selection.

## 12. Common challenges in sequence labeling

**1\. Unknown words\:** Traditional models may struggle with names or words absent from training data. Character features and pretrained contextual embeddings can help.

**2\. Long-range dependencies\:** A relevant clue may be many tokens away. BiLSTMs and Transformers can capture broader context, although neither guarantees perfect understanding.

**3\. Label imbalance\:** Many tokens may belong to the `O` class. Class weighting, careful sampling, and entity-level evaluation can help.

**4\. Inconsistent labels\:** If the training data labels the same type of expression differently, the model learns conflicting patterns.

**5\. Invalid label transitions\:** Some label sequences are structurally invalid under a particular scheme. Transition constraints or CRF decoding can help enforce consistency.

**6\. Subword alignment\:** Transformer tokenizers may split one word into multiple pieces, requiring careful alignment between word labels and subword tokens.

**7\. Overfitting\:** A model may memorize the training data without generalizing. Use validation data, regularization, and held-out testing.

**Stage 1 — Understand HMM and Viterbi**

**Stage 2 — Learn CRFs**

---

**Stage 3 — Build a BiLSTM**

Learn embeddings, hidden states, bidirectionality, padding masks, loss functions, and training loops in PyTorch.

---

**Stage 4 — Fine-tune a Transformer**

Use BERT for token classification, align word-level labels with subwords, fine-tune, and evaluate entity-level F1.

---

**Stage 5 — Compare models experimentally**

Train different approaches on the same dataset and compare accuracy, precision, recall, F1, training time, and inference speed.
